*OilWatch Analysis — the depletion thesis reaches the arsenal: what the reported exhaustion of US long-range missiles means for the war, the talks, and the shield over Gulf oil.*

---

## The constraint was never just the map

For five months, the question this war kept asking was geographic: which strait is passable, which route is safe, which facility is in range. This week the question changed shape.

Reuters reported on Monday, citing three people familiar with internal data, that the US Army has used **"virtually all"** of its long-range surface-to-surface precision missiles — the Army Tactical Missile Systems (ATACMS) and the newer Precision Strike Missiles (PrSM) — during the five-month war with Iran. The depletion had not been previously reported. A fourth source added the qualification that matters: Central Command has nearly exhausted the land-based missiles it held before the war began, but has been able to **reload from US military supplies elsewhere in the world**.

The defensive side of the ledger is thinner too. A report published last week by the Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated that between February and July about **65% of Patriot interceptors** had been expended and that **THAAD interceptor stocks were at least 38% lower** than at the war's start — figures that two of Reuters' sources say match internal US data. One source said a little under half of the global Tomahawk cruise-missile supply has been used since the war began; Reuters could not independently verify that number, and neither can we.

The White House response was a statement from President Trump saying the United States has "far more munitions than anyone in the world" and "far more than we need," with defence companies producing at record levels. The Pentagon's spokesperson said the military "has everything it needs." Those denials are part of the record and belong in it.

But the figures were reportedly circulating inside the government during tense conversations about a specific question: **how much longer can the US keep striking Iran without drawing stockpiles down to levels that limit its ability to respond to crises elsewhere?** That is not a question anyone asks about an arsenal that is comfortably sufficient.

## The shield is a stockpile too

The instinct is to read munitions depletion as de-escalatory — fewer missiles, less war. The oil market should resist reading it only one way.

The offensive missiles are one half of the story. The other half is the shield. Patriot and THAAD are the systems that detect and destroy incoming ballistic missiles, and they are among the most effective in the American arsenal. For five months, Gulf oil infrastructure — the processing plants, export terminals and pumping stations whose survival the market prices every day — has operated under the protection of exactly these systems. Drones aimed at Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province facilities were intercepted. Iranian ballistic missiles aimed at US forces were shot down. The infrastructure the world's spare barrels flow through has survived this war in meaningful part because **the interceptors kept working**.

An interceptor fired is an interceptor gone. If the CSIS estimates are right, roughly two-thirds of the Patriot stock and more than a third of the THAAD stock that existed in February no longer exists. The shield over Abqaiq, Ras Tanura, Jubail and the East–West pipeline pumping stations is not a fixed property of the map. It is an inventory — and it is being spent.

That is the two-sided read. Depletion pushes Washington toward the negotiated pause the market has been pricing all week — and it was reportedly among the reasons last week's planned offensive was shelved, though a US official disputed that account and attributed the decision to Gulf-state pressure instead; the two explanations are not mutually exclusive. But depletion also means that if the diplomacy fails and the missile exchanges resume, they resume against a thinner defensive screen than at any point in the war. The market has spent five months learning that Gulf facilities get attacked and survive. The survival rate was never a law of nature. It was a stockpile.

## This is the whole war in one story

Step back and the munitions report reads less like a new development than like the latest entry in a ledger this war has been writing since February.

Our detours analysis traced the pattern at sea: Hormuz, then Yanbu, then Bab el-Mandeb, then Suez/SUMED — each escape route inheriting the load, and then the threat, of the one before it, so that every workaround used up makes the next disruption cost more. Europe's diesel story is the same shape: commercial inventories drawn down, refinery maintenance deferred, the buffer between disruption and pump prices thinning week by week. The rivers are the same shape again — on the day the missile story broke, the Rhine recorded its lowest level since measurements began in 1880, and Romania's military was blasting rock out of the Danube riverbed to keep cooling water flowing to a nuclear plant. Buffers of every kind — routes, stocks, river depth, insurance appetite — being spent faster than anything replaces them.

The munitions are the version of that story nobody could see until now. Precision missiles cost over a million dollars each, and the ones in question take years, not months, to build in quantity. PrSM stocks were low to begin with — it is a new munition, with large deliveries ordered for 2027 — and ATACMS is being phased out, its production lines shifting to the newer missile. The Pentagon and Raytheon have reached a tentative multi-year agreement to boost Tomahawk production, and Trump is right that output is at record levels. But analysts' caution is the one that matters: record production and adequate production are different claims, and a prolonged war tests the second, not the first.

The strategic anxiety in the Reuters report goes beyond this war. The sources' stated worry is that depleted stockpiles limit the ability to deter Russia and China — the same class of long-range, stand-off weapons that would matter most against an adversary with serious air defences. Five months of Iran has consumed inventory that was, in the planning documents, meant for something else. Wars do not only spend what they are given. They spend what was reserved for the next one.

## The honest limits

The sourcing deserves its own paragraph. The core claims rest on anonymous sources describing internal data Reuters has not seen, cross-checked against a public CSIS estimate that two of those sources endorse. The White House and Pentagon deny any readiness gap, CENTCOM has demonstrably been able to reload from global stocks, and the Tomahawk figure is single-sourced and unverified. Nobody outside the government knows the absolute numbers — the sources declined to give them — and "virtually all" of a stockpile whose size is classified is a direction, not a quantity. We treat this as well-sourced reporting of a real internal concern, not as an audited inventory.

Nor is depletion the same as incapacity. The US retains air power, naval strike, and the ability to concentrate munitions from other theatres. The constraint the sources describe is about **sustained tempo and simultaneous readiness** — how long the current campaign can continue while keeping enough in reserve for everywhere else — not about whether any single strike can still be flown.

And there is a fact in the story that cuts against the drama: the political system is responding to the constraint. The shelved offensive, the negotiating track, the production agreements — these are what an arsenal-aware government does. The buffer is being managed, not ignored. Whether it is being managed in time is the open question.

## What we are watching

**Strike tempo against the talks.** If US operations stay paused while the Oman channel works, the munitions constraint and the diplomacy are pulling the same direction. If strikes resume at scale, watch how quickly — and with what — the tempo is sustained.

**The interceptor screen in action.** The grim, observable test: whether Iranian missiles and drones start getting through to targets that five months of precedent says get defended. A successful strike on defended Gulf energy infrastructure would be the market's first visible evidence that the shield has thinned.

**Resupply signals.** Patriot and THAAD replenishment rates, the Tomahawk multi-year agreement moving from tentative to signed, PrSM deliveries pulled forward from 2027. Production announcements are easy; delivery schedules are the tell.

**Allied stockpiles.** ATACMS has been a mainstay of Ukraine's deep-strike capability. If US stocks are as low as reported, the pressure to choose between theatres arrives quickly.

**Congressional friction.** No authorization for the use of force has been submitted for a five-month war now measurably consuming the national arsenal. The stockpile data gives that debate a number to organise around.

---

Every analysis we have published since February has, in the end, been about the same discovery: the systems that looked like fixed features of the world — routes, reserves, river depth, refining capacity — were actually inventories, and inventories run down. This week the discovery reached the arsenal itself.

The war's most important numbers were never the barrel counts or the tanker transits. They were the balances nobody publishes: how many interceptors are left, how many safe routes, how many days of diesel, how many centimetres of river. Those balances are all moving the same direction. The question the market is really pricing — beneath the talks, beneath the pauses — is which one runs out first.
