# Europe Has a Fuel Problem — Not Just an Oil-Price Problem

**30 July 2026**

Europe has spent much of this year watching the price of crude oil.

It may be watching the wrong number.

Brent has again jumped above $90 as fighting between the United States and Iran intensifies. But underneath the violent movements in crude futures, a more persistent problem is developing.

**Europe is short of comfortable fuel buffers.**

Diesel inventories are at their lowest level since 2022. Russia is preparing to extend its diesel export ban. European and American refineries are already running hard. Middle Eastern refining and shipping remain disrupted. Kazakhstan's principal export route has again stopped loading oil after another tanker attack.

And winter is approaching with European gas storage only around 55% full.

The immediate danger is not that Europe suddenly runs out of crude.

It is that several individually manageable problems are beginning to overlap.

Brent rose another 1.6% on Thursday morning after gaining roughly 8% on Wednesday.

The move follows renewed U.S. strikes on Iran and joint U.S.–Saudi attacks on Iran-backed forces in Iraq after drones launched from Iraqi territory targeted Saudi oil facilities.

Note the Brent–WTI spread. It has widened to roughly $7.3 from under $5 in a day. That is not noise: waterborne Brent carries the Hormuz risk premium while landlocked WTI largely does not, so **the gap between them is itself a measure of chokepoint stress.**

The market is once again swinging between two competing ideas: **the war is worsening**, and **physical oil continues to find ways through.** That tension explains why $5–$10 daily moves in crude have become common.

## Hormuz Is Not Normal — Even When a Ship Gets Through

One development overnight deserves particular attention.

The **Al Areesh**, a QatarEnergy-controlled LNG tanker, passed through the Strait of Hormuz after receiving permission to use an Iran-designated route. It was the first QatarEnergy-controlled LNG tanker to exit Hormuz in almost three weeks.

That is positive. It is not the same thing as normal navigation having returned.

Before the war, Hormuz handled roughly one-fifth of globally traded oil and LNG. Individual vessels now obtaining passage through approved routes demonstrate that the Strait is physically navigable **by permission**; they do not demonstrate that commercial shipping has returned to anything resembling its former scale.

For EuroOilWatch the useful question is no longer *is Hormuz technically open or closed?*

It is: **how many barrels and LNG cargoes are actually getting through, how reliably, and at what cost?**

That is the number that eventually reaches European consumers.

## Brent: From $105 Physical to $84 Futures — and Back Above $92

The last week has also demonstrated why we need to distinguish carefully between **physical Dated Brent** and the futures screen.

On 23 July, Reuters reported **Dated Brent at $105.70/bbl**, while physical Forties reached $108.77 as buyers scrambled for prompt Atlantic Basin crude. That was a genuine physical premium caused by tight immediate supply.

But the premium did not remain there. Publicly available Argus data subsequently showed North Sea Dated falling sharply, to approximately the mid-$80s by 28 July as the immediate panic eased. The Australian Institute of Petroleum publishes those Argus North Sea Dated assessments as an end-of-day physical benchmark.

Then futures reversed again. Brent fell as low as **$84.09 on Tuesday**, before rising sharply on Wednesday and reaching **$92.22 Thursday morning**.

**Our data rule.** We will not carry forward an old Dated Brent price as though it were current. Our physical-price display always shows the date of the underlying assessment. The last exact independently verified Reuters Dated Brent print remains **$105.70 — 23 July**; the later public Argus chart establishes that physical crude then fell sharply, but until we have another exact assessment we will not invent precision.

In this market, the date attached to a price matters almost as much as the price.

## Europe's Bigger Problem Is Diesel

Crude headlines attract attention. Diesel may be the more important European story.

Reuters reports that **European diesel inventories are now at their lowest level since 2022.**

Europe relies heavily on imported middle distillates. Diesel powers road freight, agriculture, construction and industry, while closely related heating oil becomes increasingly important as winter approaches. Normally Europe would use the summer to build those stocks. Instead, inventories have been falling.

Middle Eastern exports have been disrupted. Russian product supply has collapsed. Asian refiners have struggled to restore normal throughput because Gulf crude movements remain constrained. And refineries in Europe and the United States are already expected to run close to maximum practical rates through the third quarter.

This is precisely why crude prices can fall while consumers still face expensive fuel.

**A barrel of crude is not a litre of diesel.** Refining, transport, specification and location all matter.

## Russia Is Preparing to Extend Its Diesel Export Ban

Russia introduced a diesel export ban from **8 July to 31 July** after repeated Ukrainian drone attacks disrupted refineries and contributed to domestic shortages and rising fuel prices.

Reuters reported on Wednesday that Moscow is preparing to extend the restriction by another month, though it could lift the ban around mid-August if domestic supply improves. Russia has already restricted gasoline and jet-fuel exports, with gasoline restrictions expected to remain through the end of the year.

The scale of the disruption is striking. Russian diesel and gasoil exports averaged approximately **817,000 b/d in 2025**, fell to about **400,000 b/d in June 2026**, and only **234,000 b/d during 1–10 July.**

Europe no longer depends directly on Russian diesel in the way it did before sanctions. That does **not** mean Europe is insulated.

Brazil, Turkey and other traditional buyers of Russian product are now competing for alternative supplies — particularly U.S. cargoes — in the same global market as European importers. Remove Russian diesel from the global pool and everyone else competes harder for what remains.

That is how sanctions, refinery attacks and Russian domestic shortages still affect a driver filling a diesel car in France, Germany or Spain without a Russian molecule ever entering the tank.

## Refiners Are Making Money Because Fuel Is Scarce

Reuters reported last week that fuel margins had reached **record levels in Europe and the United States** as refining capacity struggled to respond to simultaneous disruptions in Russia and the Middle East. Asian gasoil and jet-fuel margins had risen above **$65/bbl**, against just over $20 before the war.

TotalEnergies' second-quarter results provide a real-world indicator: the French major reported its strongest quarterly earnings in nearly three years, with its refining segment up **362% year-on-year**.

That does not mean European refiners are creating the shortage. It means the market is paying very large premiums for every available unit of refining capacity.

When refining margins rise dramatically while crude itself falls, the message is straightforward: **the bottleneck has moved downstream.**

## Kazakhstan: Europe's Other Supply Route Is Being Hit

The Middle East is not Europe's only crude-supply problem.

The **Caspian Pipeline Consortium**, which carries most Kazakh oil to the Black Sea, suspended loadings again on Thursday after another drone attack on a tanker — following repeated attacks and shutdowns through July.

CPC transports around **80% of Kazakhstan's oil exports** and handles roughly **2% of global crude supply.** Earlier disruption forced Kazakhstan to cut production sharply as storage filled. At the Tengiz field, output fell from around **925,000 b/d to 406,000 b/d.** By 27 July, Kazakhstan's total daily production had temporarily fallen to about **1 million b/d**, less than half June's average of 2.16 million b/d.

CPC crude matters to Europe specifically because much of it enters the Mediterranean and European refining system. And the shorthand "Russian Black Sea terminal" understates who is affected: this is predominantly **Kazakh crude**, including barrels produced by Chevron and ExxonMobil.

Europe therefore faces supply disruption from two geographically separate conflicts at once — **Middle Eastern crude constrained by Hormuz and the Red Sea**, and **Caspian crude constrained by attacks around the Black Sea export route.**

That is a much less comfortable position than Brent alone suggests.

## The EU Is Expanding Its Russian-Oil Enforcement Perimeter

Brussels also widened sanctions enforcement this week, imposing a transaction ban on **Georgia's Kulevi refinery** — the first such action against a major Georgian company — as part of the 21st sanctions package. The refinery had processed Russian crude. The restriction takes effect in six months.

Research cited by Reuters found Kulevi received six Russian crude shipments between October 2025 and May 2026. During that period, refined products from Kulevi were shipped to countries including **Spain and Bulgaria**. The EU and United States imported approximately **€811 million of petroleum products** from Kulevi port between February 2023 and February 2026.

The direction of EU policy is becoming clear: it is no longer enough for Russian crude to be refined somewhere outside Russia and return with a different customs identity. Brussels is increasingly targeting the **refinery and trading infrastructure used to circumvent the economic intention of the sanctions regime.**

For European buyers, that narrows the universe of permissible supply further.

## Europe Is Entering the Winter Build Season With Thin Buffers

European underground gas storage is only around **55% full** — the lowest level for this point in the year since 2021, according to LSEG data cited by Reuters.

July LNG imports are on course to total only around **6.3 million tonnes**, the lowest since September 2024. European benchmark gas prices peaked above **€60/MWh** on 24 July before easing to around **€58**.

Meanwhile Europe is entering the period when heating-oil inventories should be accumulating — except diesel inventories are instead at multi-year lows.

Oil and gas are different markets, but their risks compound. If gas is expensive or unavailable, more oil products can substitute in some industrial and heating applications. If diesel and heating-oil stocks are already low, that substitution becomes harder.

Europe therefore enters the second half of 2026 with **less redundancy between fuels than headline aggregate energy figures suggest.**

## Italy Shows What Qatari Disruption Looks Like in Practice

Italian utility Edison provided an unusually clear illustration on Thursday, again cutting its 2026 earnings forecast because of weak hydroelectric output and disrupted Qatari gas deliveries.

QatarEnergy normally supplies Edison approximately **6.4 billion cubic metres of gas a year — around 10% of Italian gas consumption.** QatarEnergy has cancelled or deferred **24 LNG cargoes scheduled for Edison between April and September**. Edison has so far replaced 17 of those, representing roughly 1.6 bcm.

That is important. Europe has not simply lost energy and gone without. It has been able to replace much of it.

But replacement carries a cost. Every European buyer searching the Atlantic Basin for replacement LNG is bidding against Asian buyers doing exactly the same thing. The same dynamic is now appearing in diesel.

Europe still has access to global markets. The price of that access is rising.

## Qatar Is Buying American LNG to Supply Its Own Customers

If anyone needed evidence of how distorted the global energy map has become: **Qatar is buying U.S. LNG.**

QatarEnergy — normally one of the world's largest LNG exporters — has bought **33 U.S. spot cargoes during 2026** to compensate for disruption to its own exports through Hormuz. Reuters sources estimate those cargoes are worth roughly **$1 billion.** Twenty-eight have been delivered, with another five en route.

American LNG is effectively being purchased by Qatar so that Qatar can fulfil commitments to customers its own geographically closer production cannot reliably reach.

Readers of Monday's analysis will recognise the shape of this. Russia is exporting crude while importing gasoline; Qatar is exporting LNG while importing LNG. In both cases the resource is not the constraint — **the ability to move and process it is.** That is the signature of this crisis, and it is why inventory and logistics now matter more than reserves.

For Europe, the implication is again competition. Those U.S. cargoes cannot simultaneously be delivered to Rotterdam, Dunkirk or Wilhelmshaven.

## One Piece of Good News: OPEC+ May Stop Increasing Supply

Or perhaps, depending on your perspective, one piece of bad news.

OPEC+ is expected to approve another approximately **188,000 b/d production increase for September** when seven members meet on 2 August. Reuters sources say the group is then likely to **pause further increases for three months from October.** Around **2 million b/d of cuts would remain in place.**

Until now, additional OPEC+ output has provided some protection against disrupted Gulf production. If those increases stop while Hormuz remains impaired and global fuel inventories stay tight, one of the remaining sources of incremental crude supply disappears.

That does not guarantee higher prices — demand destruction could still overwhelm supply pressure. But it removes another of the system's shock absorbers.

## European Pump Prices: A Data Warning

The latest European Commission Weekly Oil Bulletin published on the Commission website is dated **23 July.** A new bulletin is normally released on Thursday, after national price data are submitted on Wednesday.

We will therefore **not label 23 July pump-price data as current 30 July data.**

A retail price can legitimately lag crude by days or weeks because of refinery costs, wholesale contracts, taxation and inventory turnover. A stale data point cannot legitimately be presented as today's observation.

## What We Are Watching

**1. Actual Hormuz traffic** — not statements about whether the Strait is "open." The metric is vessels and barrels successfully transiting.

**2. Diesel inventories** — currently Europe's most exposed petroleum-product market.

**3. Russia's diesel ban** — a formal extension beyond 31 July would remove more supply from an already tight global distillate market.

**4. CPC and Kazakhstan** — every additional shutdown matters because Europe needs alternative crude precisely as Middle Eastern flows remain uncertain.

**5. European winter stocks** — gas storage at roughly 55% and thin heating-oil/diesel stocks leave less room for a cold autumn.

**6. Dated Brent versus futures** — physical crude temporarily carried an enormous premium; that premium then collapsed. We track the physical market separately rather than infer today's physical value from an old assessment.

## EuroOilWatch Assessment

### STATUS: TIGHT — HIGH GEOPOLITICAL AND PRODUCT-SUPPLY RISK

Europe is not running out of oil. It has diversified crude suppliers, large refining assets, strategic stocks, extensive ports and access to one of the deepest international energy markets in the world. Those advantages matter.

But the buffer is thinner than the headline supply numbers suggest.

The Middle East has disrupted crude, LNG and refined-product flows. Russian refinery damage has removed diesel from the global market. Kazakhstan's principal oil-export route has repeatedly been interrupted. European diesel inventories are at their lowest since 2022. Gas storage is only around 55% full. And refineries are already being asked to operate extremely hard.

The most important conclusion this week is therefore not that Brent is $92 rather than $84 or $105.

It is this:

> **Europe can still replace almost everything it loses — but it is increasingly having to replace several things at once.**

That is when resilience stops being a question of total resources and becomes a question of **spare capacity, logistics, time and price.**

For now the system is holding. The margin for another large shock is getting smaller.
