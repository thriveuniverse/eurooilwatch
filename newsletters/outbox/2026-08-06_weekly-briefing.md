# The Market Sees Peace. Europe Sees Empty Tanks and Dry Rivers.

Brent has fallen below $80 on hopes that the Strait of Hormuz will reopen. European diesel is still rising, gas storage is at a record seasonal low, and the Danube is no longer carrying enough water to keep nuclear plants, hydropower stations and fuel barges operating normally.

**By Jon Kelly**
**6 August 2026 · ≈7 min read · EuroOilWatch**

## Oil is falling. Europe's fuel bill is not.

The financial market believes relief is approaching.

Brent was trading at $79.91 a barrel on Thursday morning, down from about $96 on 23 July, as investors bet that negotiations between Iran and Oman will create a protected shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz. Gulf oil and condensate exports, however, remained approximately 40% below pre-war levels in July.

At European filling stations, the direction remains upward.

The European Commission's latest Weekly Oil Bulletin, covering prices on 3 August, puts the EU-weighted average at approximately:

**€1.952 a litre for petrol**
**€2.043 a litre for diesel**

Petrol rose by approximately 1.2 cents in the latest week. Diesel rose by 3.4 cents.

Since 6 July, the EU average has increased by nearly 14 cents a litre for petrol and almost 28 cents for diesel. A 55-litre diesel fill now costs approximately €112.37—more than €15 above its cost four weeks earlier. These are EuroOilWatch calculations from the Commission's current price workbook, using its consumption-weighted EU average. (The simple average of the 27 national prices — the figure shown on our dashboard — is a few cents lower, because it counts Malta equally with Germany.)

This is not a contradiction. It is a warning.

The market price of crude can fall immediately when diplomats suggest that tankers may soon be allowed through. The physical price of fuel reflects whether crude has already reached a functioning refinery, whether that refinery can turn it into diesel or petrol, and whether the resulting product can be transported into Europe.

On those measures, the system remains damaged.

## Europe has a diesel crisis inside an oil crisis

Before the Iran war, the refining component of the euro area's retail diesel price was approximately €0.10 a litre. During the first three weeks of July it reached €0.35.

For petrol, the refining component rose from approximately €0.04 a litre in February to €0.23 in July. The European Central Bank analysis behind those figures expects the refining contribution to peak during August before gradually declining.

That matters because Europe does not run on crude oil.

Its trucks, tractors, construction machinery, emergency generators and much of its industrial equipment run on diesel. Aircraft require jet fuel. Households in parts of northern Europe still depend upon heating oil.

Even if the Strait of Hormuz partially reopens, more crude supply will not instantly repair damaged refineries or restore the product inventories consumed during five months of disruption.

Diesel refining margins reached an all-time high of approximately $75 a barrel on 31 July. They have since eased to around $63, but remain more than 50% above their mid-June level. The structure of the Brent market also remains strongly backwardated, indicating that traders still place a premium on oil available immediately despite the fall in headline prices.

The market is pricing the possibility of future relief while continuing to pay heavily for fuel that physically exists today.

## Russia's fuel shortage has become Europe's problem without Europe importing Russian fuel

The strangest illustration of the refining shortage is now Russia itself.

One of the world's largest oil producers is importing increasing quantities of petrol and diesel after Ukrainian attacks and unplanned outages reduced its refinery output.

Belarus supplied Russia with a record 212,000 tonnes of petrol and 162,000 tonnes of diesel by rail in July. Russian petrol production had fallen to a level equivalent to approximately 65% of normal seasonal consumption, while diesel production was only roughly sufficient to meet domestic demand. Russia has also begun importing petrol from India, Kazakhstan and Morocco.

Moscow has responded by restricting exports of diesel, petrol and jet fuel.

Europe may no longer be a direct buyer of Russian refined products, but it still pays the consequences. Barrels that would otherwise have reached Turkey, Brazil, Africa or Latin America are removed from the international market. Those countries then compete with European buyers for alternative cargoes from the United States, India and the Middle East.

Sanctions can alter who buys a barrel. They cannot prevent a missing barrel from tightening the global market.

The result appears not only in transport costs but in monetary policy. Euro-area inflation rose to 2.9% in July, while the annual rate of energy inflation increased from 8.5% in June to 10%.

The decline in Brent will eventually help if it lasts. But a cheaper crude contract does not erase the inflation already entering European supply chains through diesel, aviation fuel, shipping, fertiliser and industrial power.

## Europe's gas buffer is disappearing before winter begins

The more serious risk may now be gas.

European storage facilities are just under 58% full. That is the lowest level for this point in the year since records began in 2011 and approximately 12 percentage points below last year.

The European Union's storage rules originally demanded 90% before winter. The target has since been made flexible — 80–90%, reachable at any point between October and December — precisely because forcing companies to buy rapidly into a tight market could drive prices even higher.

Even the lower end of that range is becoming difficult.

Forecasts reviewed by Reuters suggest Europe may reach only 67% to 76% before winter. To achieve 80%, injections would need to rise towards record rates—something analysts say may require government intervention.

The market is not naturally encouraging storage.

European gas was trading at approximately €53/MWh this week, compared with around €31 before the latest disruption. Near-term gas costs more than winter gas, meaning companies can lose money by buying fuel now, paying to store it and selling it later.

In ordinary circumstances that price signal discourages unnecessary stock accumulation. In an energy-security crisis it discourages Europe from building the reserve it may need.

The EU has more LNG terminals than it did during the 2022 crisis and consumes less gas. But LNG has changed the nature of Europe's exposure. Instead of relying primarily on pipeline supply governed by long-term arrangements, Europe must increasingly compete for globally traded cargoes with Asia.

If Hormuz remains constrained, Asian buyers must replace missing Qatari LNG from the same international market Europe needs to refill its storage.

Energy Aspects estimates that European winter gas could trade in a range of €60–€80/MWh if Hormuz traffic normalises. With no Qatari LNG and colder-than-normal weather, the average could reach €110. Building a larger end-of-winter reserve under that scenario could require prices substantially higher again.

These are scenarios, not predictions. But they show how little room remains between an affordable winter and another industrial energy shock.

## The Danube has become an energy chokepoint

Europe's third failing buffer is not stored underground or carried by tanker.

It is water.

Record-low river levels have reduced electricity output, interrupted fuel and grain transport and forced governments to import power precisely when heatwaves are driving demand higher.

Hungary's Paks nuclear plant—the source of nearly half the country's electricity—was operating at just 240 MW, or a little over 10% of capacity, after a modest rise in the Danube narrowly prevented the complete shutdown announced for 2 August. Hungarian businesses and households have been cutting peak consumption by 600–700 megawatts a day, while freight trains and decorative lighting have been suspended during the most difficult hours.

The problem extends far beyond Hungary.

Serbia's largest hydropower station has fallen to approximately 20% of capacity. Romania shut one of its two nuclear reactors and undertook emergency engineering work to redirect Danube water towards the remaining unit. French nuclear production has also been constrained by low water levels and high river temperatures.

At the same time, reduced river depth is limiting the amount carried by each barge.

Cargo movements between Rotterdam and the Rhine were approximately 10% below normal by the end of July. Oil-product and chemical tankers are particularly exposed because their deeper draught requires more water.

This creates another hidden multiplier.

When river transport fails, fuel does not merely arrive later. More of it must move by rail or road—using additional diesel in a market already suffering from a diesel shortage.

Europe is therefore being forced to replace lost nuclear and hydroelectric output with imported electricity or gas-fired generation while simultaneously replacing efficient river freight with more energy-intensive transport.

The drought is not a separate environmental story. It is part of the energy crisis.

## Three buffers are failing together

Europe has endured energy shocks before. What makes the present situation dangerous is the interaction between systems.

Its oil-products buffer has been depleted by Middle Eastern export disruption, Russian refinery damage and months of strong summer demand.

Its gas buffer is being refilled too slowly because LNG is scarce and market prices discourage storage.

Its water buffer is failing to support nuclear cooling, hydropower production and low-cost transport.

Each weakness makes the others harder to repair.

Low river levels increase the cost of transporting fuel. Reduced nuclear and hydroelectric production increases demand for gas. Higher gas and diesel prices raise industrial costs and inflation. Higher inflation limits the ability of governments and central banks to support struggling industries and consumers.

Europe's dependence is structural. More than half of the energy consumed in the EU still comes from imported fossil fuels, including more than 80% of its gas and over 95% of its oil.

The continent has built more renewable generation, LNG terminals and cross-border connections. Those investments matter. They have prevented the current disruption from becoming an immediate continent-wide shortage.

But additional import capacity cannot guarantee affordable supply when every buyer is chasing the same cargoes.

## What matters next

**Physical Hormuz traffic.** Diplomatic statements matter only when sustained tanker and LNG movements begin to recover.

**European diesel prices.** If Brent remains near $80 while diesel stays above €2 a litre, the shortage remains in the refining and distribution system.

**Gas-storage injections.** Europe needs a visible acceleration during August. A continuing rise of one or two percentage points a week will leave the continent dangerously exposed.

**The Danube and Rhine.** River levels now affect electricity generation, refinery logistics, grain exports and fuel transport simultaneously.

**Government intervention.** The EU must decide whether gas storage is a commercial decision or a strategic reserve. It cannot insist that storage is essential while relying on price signals that tell companies not to fill it.

## The weekly picture

The fall in Brent is welcome.

A durable agreement that restores traffic through Hormuz would ease the immediate pressure on crude supply, reduce freight and insurance costs and eventually feed through to European motorists and industry.

But Europe should not mistake the repricing of a futures contract for the restoration of its energy system.

Petrol and diesel are still rising. Gas storage is at a record seasonal low. Russia is importing fuel. Nuclear plants are losing cooling water. Barges are carrying less cargo. Inflation is moving upward again.

The market sees a possible peace agreement.

Europe is entering August with fewer physical buffers than it had when the last ceasefire failed.
